The Long Term Effects of Restrictive Racial Covenants: Evidence from San José (work in progress)

By Jake Wilde

“It is hereby covenanted and agreed that no part of said premises, or any building thereon, shall be used or occupied by any person or persons not of the white or Caucasian race," so goes the text of a racial restrictive covenant recorded to the deed of a home in the Naglee Park neighborhood of San Jose. Racial covenants—contractual property agreements prohibiting homeowners from selling or leasing to non-white buyers—such as the one from Naglee Park, emerged as the preeminent tool for establishing and reinforcing residential racial segregation in the United States during the early 20th century. Racial covenants were remarkably popular with the nation’s home builders, and only began to fall out of use after Shelley v. Kraemer rendered them unconstitutional and unenforceable in 1948. However, this ruling did not mark the true end of the racial covenant’s reign. Nearly 80 years later, it's clear segregationists picked the right tool for the job: racial covenants remain stalwart enforcers of residential segregation.

I analyzed the demographics of 57 San Jose neighborhoods across nine decades to estimate differences in racial composition between covenanted and non-covenanted neighborhoods over time. The evidence I found points to a distinct and growing divide in the racial demographics of covenanted and non-covenanted neighborhoods. Compared to neighborhoods lacking racial restrictions, those areas with restrictions tend to have smaller Asian and Black populations. This difference has only grown since 1948.

In 2020, neighborhoods with covenants had an estimated 6.54 percentage point smaller share of Asian residents than neighborhoods without covenants. The year 2020 marks the largest divide between covenanted and non-covenanted neighborhoods in San Jose’s history. If left unchecked, the divide may continue to grow.
